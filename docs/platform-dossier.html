Part I

The business

What it is

HealthEMR is the system a telehealth company runs its clinical operation on. A patient answers a questionnaire; a licensed clinician reviews it and either prescribes or declines; an approved prescription is transmitted to a pharmacy, dispensed, and shipped. HealthEMR holds every step of that, along with the record it produces.

The distinguishing choice is that it is multi-tenant. One installation serves many telehealth brands at once. Each brand — called a client account in the product — has its own patients, its own clinician roster, its own pharmacy relationships, its own catalogue and pricing. None of it is visible to any other brand. The platform operator sits above all of them and can see everything, which is what makes the operator a business rather than a piece of software: they carry the compliance burden and the pharmacy relationships so that each brand does not have to.

It replaces Beluga Health as the clinical back end. Where a brand previously rented a black box and accepted whatever it reported, HealthEMR gives the operator the database, the audit trail, the pharmacy integrations and the clinician network directly.

Who uses it

Five roles, each with its own sign-in, its own area of the application, and its own slice of the data. Nobody can reach another role's area — this is checked by the browser before a page renders and again by the API before any data is returned.

Worth knowing

A clinician is not owned by a brand. One licensed physician can take visits from several client accounts, and the platform bills each account for the visits that clinician handled on its behalf. This is the main reason the operator is worth paying: a new brand gets an existing, credentialled, multi-state clinician network on day one.

A day in each seat

The patient

Arrives from a brand's marketing site, answers a medical questionnaire, and uploads a photo of their ID. They see their visit move through review, a prescription when one is written, and tracking when it ships. If the clinician needs something more — a clearer photograph, an answer they left blank — the patient gets a message and the visit waits for them.

The clinician

Opens a queue of visits already filtered to the ones they are licensed and credentialled to handle. Each shows the questionnaire, the patient's history, allergies, and what is being requested. They approve, decline with a reason, or ask the patient for more. Approving signs the prescription; signing is what starts everything downstream.

The pharmacy

Receives signed prescriptions as orders, with directions, quantity, days supply and the shipping address. They acknowledge, dispense, and report a carrier and tracking number back. Most partners are integrated, so this happens machine-to-machine and the dispensary screen is for exceptions.

The brand (admin)

Watches its own funnel: visits in, approval rate, what shipped, what is stuck, what it owes. It manages its own catalogue — which medications it offers, at what price — and its own staff.

The operator (super admin)

Runs the platform. Onboards new brands, credentials clinicians, contracts pharmacies, sets the catalogue the brands draw from, watches revenue and margin across everything, and investigates anything stuck. The operator is also the only role that can see the audit log, add an outbound webhook, or hold a pharmacy credential.

How the money works

Three numbers travel with every prescribed line, and keeping them separate is what makes the platform's margin visible:

Cost of goods
What the pharmacy charges the platform to dispense that product. Negotiated per pharmacy, per product.
Sell price
What the platform charges the client account. Set per account, so two brands can pay differently for the same medication.
Clinician fee
What the reviewing clinician earns for the decision. Earned on the visit, not on the product.

Margin is sell price minus cost of goods minus clinician fee, and the operator can see it per product, per account and per month. Invoices are raised against client accounts, not against patients — the brand bills its own customers however it likes, and settles with the platform.

A decision worth understanding

A visit that results in two medications from two different categories is charged as two visits, because two clinical decisions were made and two clinician fees were earned. A visit that results in two medications within one category is one visit. The billing follows the clinical work, not the shipment.

Part II

Security & HIPAA

What we defend against

Every control below exists for a named failure. Listing the failure first is the only way to tell a real control from a box being ticked.

Cross-tenant leak
One brand reads another brand's patients. The classic multi-tenant breach, and almost always a forgotten WHERE clause.
Wrong chart
A clinician is shown a patient who is not theirs — even for 200 ms. In an EMR that is an incident, not a flicker.
Quiet insider access
Someone with legitimate credentials reads records they have no reason to read, and nothing records it.
Stolen database
A backup or a disk leaves the building with identifiable patients in it.
Rewritten history
Someone edits or deletes the record of what they did.
Token theft from the browser
A script on the page reads the access token and uses it elsewhere.
Leak on the way out
PHI is sent to a pharmacy, a CRM or a log that should not have received it.

Identity and access

The access token never reaches browser JavaScript

The browser talks only to the Next.js server, at /api/bff/…. That server holds the session, attaches the API access token, and forwards the request. The token lives in an httpOnly cookie the page cannot read, so a cross-site scripting bug — the realistic way a token is stolen — yields nothing reusable. This is a back end for front end, and it is the reason the architecture has an extra hop.

Why not just call the API directly

Calling the API from the browser means the token is in JavaScript's reach, and because the API is on a different origin, the cookie protections that would otherwise help (SameSite=Strict) are not available. The BFF hop is cheap — a few milliseconds on the same host — and it is what keeps the credential out of the page entirely.

Roles are enforced twice, independently

Before any HTML is sent, middleware checks that the signed-in role owns the route being requested and redirects if not — so a patient never briefly renders a clinician's queue. Then the API checks again, on its own, for every request. The first is user experience; the second is the boundary. Both are verified for all twenty-five role/area combinations on every test run.

Sign-in cannot be used to lock colleagues out

Failed sign-ins are counted per account, read from the audit log, rather than per IP address. Counting per IP is the intuitive design and it is wrong: ten guesses at one person's account would lock out everyone else in the same office behind the same address.

Tenant isolation

Isolation is not left to whoever writes the query. The current tenant is put into request-scoped storage when the request is authenticated, and a Prisma client extension adds the tenant condition to every read and write automatically. A developer who forgets is not able to leak: the filter is applied underneath them.

Crossing that boundary is possible, but only deliberately and visibly, through a function named runWithoutTenantScope() — used by the platform operator's own screens and by background jobs that legitimately span accounts. The name is the point: it is greppable, and it reads as an exception at the call site.

PHI at rest

Identifying fields are encrypted in the application before they reach the database, with AES-256-GCM — authenticated encryption, so a tampered value fails to decrypt instead of quietly returning something wrong. This sits on top of whatever the database and disk already do; the point is that a stolen dump, a leaked backup or a compromised read-replica is not a readable patient list.

Stored values carry a version prefix (phi.v1:), so keys can be rotated by writing new rows in a newer scheme while old rows still decrypt. The key is 32 bytes, supplied by environment, and the service refuses to start with a key of the wrong length rather than silently using a weak one. If no key is configured at all it logs a loud warning and stores plaintext — acceptable only on a developer's laptop, and it says so.

Operator responsibility

PHI_ENCRYPTION_KEY must come from a real secret manager in production, and must be backed up separately from the database. A database restored without its key is unreadable; a database and key stolen together are readable. They belong in different places.

The audit trail

Forty-five kinds of action are recorded: sign-ins, record views, approvals, refusals, prescription signings, transmissions, exports, permission changes, webhook changes. Each entry stores who acted, which account they acted for, what they touched, from which address, and when.

It is tamper-evident, not merely append-only

Each row carries a monotonic sequence and a hash computed over its own content plus the previous row's hash. Editing an old entry changes its hash, which breaks every hash after it; deleting one leaves a gap in the sequence. Both are detectable, and there is a verification routine that walks the chain and reports where it first breaks.

Attribution is frozen at write time: the actor's email and the account's slug are copied into the row as text rather than only referenced by foreign key. The question "who did this" is asked years later, often precisely because the account has since been deleted — and deleting an account must not be able to rewrite history by proxy.

Break the glass

The platform operator can read any record, because someone has to be able to investigate. Every such access is recorded as an access by the operator to a specific patient. The control is not prevention — it is that the reading is visible afterwards, to someone whose job is to look.

Leaving the building

To pharmacies

Only what is needed to dispense: the patient's name and shipping address, the product, directions, quantity, days supply, and the prescriber's identity and licence. Not the questionnaire, not the history, not the record of other treatments. Credentials for each pharmacy are held encrypted and are readable only by the platform operator.

To a client's CRM, by webhook

A client account can receive the events for its own patients — visit received, approved, denied, prescription signed, order submitted, shipped, delivered — as HTTP POSTs to endpoints it controls. Several endpoints per account are supported.

  • Only the platform operator can add, change or remove a webhook. A client cannot configure its own, because a webhook is an instruction to send PHI somewhere and that decision belongs with whoever signed the agreement.
  • Each delivery carries the client's own bearer token and an HMAC signature over the exact bytes sent, timestamped, so a replay with a different body fails verification.
  • The signing secret is shown to the operator once, at creation.
  • Failures are retried on a backoff rather than hammered, and every configuration change is audited.
  • A test delivery uses a synthetic payload, never a real patient's.

To logs

Patient identifiers are not written into application logs. Log lines identify a visit or an order by its own id, which is meaningless without database access.

Honest gaps

HIPAA is not a property of source code, and no codebase can claim compliance on its own. These are the things a buyer must close outside the application:

  1. Business Associate Agreements with the hosting provider, the database host, the email provider, and every pharmacy partner. Without them the technical controls do not matter legally.
  2. Email delivery is a development stub. Invitations are generated and recorded but never sent. A provider must be wired in — and that provider handles addresses, so it needs a BAA too.
  3. Key management. The encryption key is read from environment. In production it belongs in a managed secret store with rotation and separate backup.
  4. Backup and recovery drills. Encrypted backups are necessary; a restore that has actually been rehearsed is what makes them real.
  5. Workforce controls. Training, sanction policy, and access reviews are organisational, and the audit log is the evidence they are working — but someone has to read it.
  6. Penetration test. The automated suite proves the controls behave as designed. It cannot tell you what was not designed.
Part III

Technical

The shape of it

A Turborepo monorepo with two deployable applications and four shared packages. The shared type package is what keeps the two halves honest.

PackageWhat it isNotes

Zod as the single source of truth

Each request and response shape is declared once as a Zod schema and turned into a NestJS DTO. That one declaration validates the request at the boundary, generates the OpenAPI document, and types the browser's calls. The alternative — an interface, a validator and a doc comment that must be kept in agreement — is where drift lives.

The eighteen contexts

The API is organised by what the business does, not by technical layer: intake, practitioners, prescribing, pharmacy, patients, billing, catalog, messaging, notifications, onboarding, identity, tenancy, webhooks, partner-api, admin, super-admin, jobs, health. A change to how prescriptions are signed touches one directory.

One request, end to end

The visit lifecycle

A visit is the unit of clinical work. It moves through a fixed set of stages, and each transition is an event other parts of the system react to rather than a function one part calls on another.

Events are what keep this extensible. When a prescription is signed, the prescribing context publishes prescription.signed and stops caring. Fulfilment picks it up and transmits; messaging posts a note into the patient's thread; the webhook sender pushes it to whichever CRMs that client account configured; billing records the clinician's fee. Adding a sixth subscriber does not touch the fifth.

The routing engine

A visit must reach a clinician who is licensed in the patient's state and credentialled for the treatment category. The engine loads the eligible roster once, then places the visit.

  1. Prefer one clinician for the whole visit. If a single clinician covers every requested line, they get all of it — one reviewer, one conversation with the patient, one decision.
  2. Otherwise split by line. Each medication line goes to someone who can take it. A visit spanning two categories may be reviewed by two clinicians, and is charged as two visits, because two decisions were made.
  3. Distinguish "nobody is licensed for this" from "everybody is busy". The first is structural and needs the operator to recruit; the second resolves itself. The verdict is judged on the line that actually failed — an earlier version accumulated eligibility across lines and reported a permanently unplaceable visit as a temporary queue.

Attempts are logged, de-duplicated so one verdict is not recorded a dozen times, and a visit nobody can take surfaces on the operator's stuck screen rather than disappearing.

Multi-medication visits

One visit can request several medications. This is the case that complicates everything downstream, so it is worth stating precisely how it resolves:

Two products, one categoryOne clinician, one decisionOne visitOne order if one pharmacy stocks both, otherwise one per pharmacy

The rule underneath all of it: a line is the unit of clinical decision, an order is the unit of fulfilment, and they do not have to line up. Two lines may ship as one parcel; one line may be the only thing that ships at all.

Identifiers, which are easy to confuse

medId is the platform's own identifier for a product. kitCode is the pharmacy's identifier for their version of it. They are deliberately separate: the same clinical product has different codes at different pharmacies, and conflating them silently sends the wrong item.

Pharmacy integration

Two pharmacies are modelled on LifeFile, the vendor API used by First Choice and Hallandale. The integration builds the vendor's XML order from the signed prescription, transmits it, and records the vendor's identifiers against the order. A generic HTTP adapter covers partners who are not on LifeFile.

The payload has been checked field by field against the vendor's published specification and is inspectable in the application before anything is sent.

Deliberate safety catch

Transmission to the live LifeFile endpoint is disabled. Hallandale's catalogue is seeded with placeholder medication identifiers and the pharmacy record is switched off. Nothing in this installation can send an order to a real pharmacy until someone turns it on knowingly — which is the correct posture while the catalogue is still being reconciled.

Three known mismatches remain to fix before enabling: message.id should be an integer, shipping.service should be the vendor's numeric service code, and patient.city is clipped at 30 characters where the vendor allows 100.

Messaging

Patients and clinicians hold a conversation attached to the patient's record. A clinician reviewing a visit can open a thread to ask for a clearer photograph or a missing answer, and the visit waits on the patient until they reply.

Delivery is live

Messages arrive over a Socket.IO connection while the page is open — no reload, no polling. This is verified on every test run by two real browser sessions in one conversation, each of which must see the other's message appear, with a marker on the document proving neither page was replaced.

Authenticating a socket behind a BFF

The security design creates a specific problem: the browser cannot open an authenticated socket, because it does not have the access token and never should. The solution is a chat ticket — the page asks the BFF, which asks the API, which mints a JWT that is valid for sixty seconds and is good for nothing except opening a chat socket. If it leaks, it expires before it is useful and it cannot be used against any other endpoint.

Membership is the authorisation model: joining a conversation's room requires being a participant who has not left it. Being a platform operator is not sufficient, which is deliberate — reading a patient's messages should mean being in the conversation, not outranking it.

Outbound webhooks

Each client account can have several endpoints. Every delivery carries the client's bearer token and an HMAC signature over the exact body sent, with a timestamp, so the receiver can prove the payload is ours and unmodified. Only the platform operator can create or change one, and every change is audited. Delivery failures back off and retry rather than being lost or repeated aggressively.

Lists, search and filters

Every table filters on the server. A table on screen is one page of a much larger set, so filtering the rows already loaded would search a fraction of the data and confidently report nothing found.

Each endpoint declares which columns may be filtered and how — text, exact, date, number, boolean, list, presence, or name. Anything not declared is refused, so a filter cannot be used to probe an unindexed column or a field the view does not show. The declaration generates the query parameters, so a filter that is offered is one the endpoint actually accepts.

The name kind, and the bug it exists for

A column headed "Patient" shows Elena Marsh, but the name is stored as two fields. A filter matching only the surname returns nothing for what the reader can plainly see on screen — a 200 response with the wrong rows, which is worse than an error because nothing looks broken. The name kind searches every field a displayed value is composed from, requiring each typed word to match somewhere, so both "Elena Marsh" and "Marsh Elena" find her.

The same class of bug was found in five more places by round-tripping every filter against real rows: the category column showed Weight Loss but compared the slug weightloss; the licence column showed AZ-12345 (AZ) but searched only the number; "Ship to" showed a whole address but searched only the city. Pasted punctuation is now trimmed from each word, so copying a cell and dropping it into the box works.

How this stays true

Two verifications run against the live API. The first sends all 159 declared filters and requires a 200 — this catches a mistyped model path, which TypeScript cannot, because the paths are strings. The second takes a real row, reads the value the column displays, types it into the filter, and requires the same row back. 143 of 159 round-trip today; the remaining 16 are columns with no data in the current dataset, and are reported as unexercised rather than counted as passes.

In the browser, every column offers either a control or a dash that explains itself — the dash means the column is calculated for the page (a running total, an average, a rendered sentence) and so has nothing for a database filter to match. A blank cell would read as an oversight; this is checked per column, per table, on every run.

How it is tested

Routing verdicts, the audit hash chain, filter clause construction, SIG composition, LifeFile payload shape, webhook signing.
89 end-to-end tests
Against a real database: intake to delivery, a visit shared across accounts, onboarding to a live account, and the governance suite that re-verifies the audit chain after everything the tests did to it.

Real Chrome, real sessions. Accessibility, dead links, every role's route guards, column filters per table, live chat between two people, loading states, the LifeFile payload screen, webhooks.

Every declared filter answers, and every filter returns the row its value came from.

The route-guard check is the one worth singling out: it signs in as each of the five roles and attempts all four areas it does not own, requiring a redirect in the browser and a refusal from the API — twenty-five combinations, both layers, every run.

One trap

Do not run next build while the development server is running: both write .next, and the development server is left serving a half-replaced build. The symptom is a dozen unrelated checks failing at once. Stop the dev server, build, then restart it.

Weak points

Where this would strain first, in the order it would happen.

  1. The single Postgres instance. Reads are indexed and fast, but everything shares one primary. The first move at scale is a read replica for the operator's cross-tenant reporting, which is the heaviest and least latency-sensitive work.
  2. Events are in-process. The event bus is in memory, so a crash between "prescription signed" and "order transmitted" loses the transmission. It is recoverable from state, but a durable queue is what this wants before high volume.
  3. Sockets are per-instance. Chat rooms live in one process. Running more than one API instance needs a shared adapter (Redis) or messages reach only the users connected to the same instance.
  4. Encrypted fields cannot be searched. Encryption is what makes a stolen dump useless; it also means those fields cannot be filtered on. Anything that must be searchable needs a deterministic blind index, which is a deliberate trade, not an oversight to fix casually.
  5. Email is not wired. Until a provider is connected, invitations never arrive and every new account must be activated by hand.
  6. The audit log grows without bound. Correct — it is the evidence — but it needs a partitioning and archival plan before it is the largest table in the database.
